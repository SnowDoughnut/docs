---
title: "Brand overview"
description: "A fill-in workbook for the factual base of the brand: what it sells, who buys it, what problems it solves, and who it competes with. Fill this in before positioning, so every document after it argues from the same facts."
---

_Fill in this workbook as you go. Each section has a "Concept review" dropdown underneath it. These explain the concept behind the section, not a rule to follow. Your brand might land somewhere different than the examples show, and that's fine._

_Examples throughout use Doughnut Labs, a SaaS company that sells disruptive Doughnut Technology. Delete the grayed example text as you fill each section in._

<Note>
  This workbook records what is true about the business. [Positioning](/positioning) decides what to claim about it. Fill this page in first, because an argument about a claim runs on taste until the facts underneath it are written down.
</Note>

<Steps>
  <Step title="Fill in the six core sections in order">
    Each one narrows the picture. Competitors in section 6 are easier to name once you have written down who buys and what problem they are buying against.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Write down only what someone confirmed or you read">
    Every line comes from a person at the company, or from a page you opened. A plausible-sounding fact that came from neither is the one that ends up in an ad.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Translate the marketing language">
    Where the website says the product reimagines something, write down what it does. This page is read by people who have to build campaigns from it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add the optional sections only where they apply">
    Skip the rest, and delete them before you save the filled-in version.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Revisit when the business changes">
    A new product, a new market, or a competitor entering the set dates this page. A campaign underperforming does not.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
  Nothing on this page is a guess. If you cannot trace a line to a person or a page, ask instead of filling it in. Guesses are indistinguishable from facts once someone else reads the finished document, and they get quoted back to you in a client call.
</Warning>

## Core sections

### 1. Overview

**In two to four sentences: what does the company do, who for, and how long has it been doing it?**

_E.g. Doughnut Labs sells process automation software to operations teams. It turns a process that runs on a spreadsheet into one that runs on its own, set up by the person who owns the process rather than by IT. It has been selling to ops teams since 2019._

| Fact | Fill in |
| :-- | :-- |
| Category a buyer would file us under | _E.g. Process automation software_ |
| In business since | _E.g. 2019_ |
| Where the company sells from | _E.g. Remote team, contracting entity in Ireland_ |
| Filled in by | _E.g. Priya, marketing_ |
| Date filled in | _E.g. 4 March 2026_ |
| Confirmed with | _E.g. Sam, founder, on the call of 2 March_ |

<Accordion title="Concept review: Overview">
  This paragraph is the first thing a new hire, a freelancer, or an agency reads about the business, and it usually has to survive being read once. Everything in it is checkable: what the company sells, who to, and since when.

  Skipping it is common, because to the people who have been there five years it reads as obvious. The cost lands somewhere else. Someone briefing a campaign in month one reconstructs the business from the homepage, and a homepage is written to persuade rather than to describe, so the reconstruction comes out wrong in ways nobody catches until the copy is in review.

  Recording who confirmed it and when does a second job. Facts here expire quietly. A line that was accurate in March survives a product launch unnoticed unless there is a name and a date next to it.
</Accordion>

### 2. Who we serve

**Who buys this today? Describe the buyer, and what separates them from everyone else the company could sell to.**

_E.g. Operations leads at companies of 50 to 500 people. They own a process personally, they feel it breaking, and they can buy a tool on a card without a procurement cycle._

| Field | Fill in |
| :-- | :-- |
| Customer base as it stands | _E.g. About 80% software companies, 20% agencies_ |
| How they buy | _E.g. Self-serve trial, then a card. No security review below 200 seats_ |
| Industry experience we can point to | _E.g. Seven years of ops teams, roughly 1,400 accounts_ |
| Buyer we do not want | _E.g. Enterprise buyers who need SSO and an audit trail. The product does not have either yet_ |

<Accordion title="Concept review: Who we serve">
  Two questions hide inside this one, and they have different answers. Who buys the product today is a description, drawn from the customer list. Who the company wants to sell to is a decision, and it belongs in [positioning](/positioning#1-target).

  Filling in the descriptive version first is what makes the gap between them visible. Sometimes the two match and there is nothing to discuss. Sometimes a company that describes itself as selling to enterprise turns out to have a customer list of 40-person startups, and that gap is the thing to settle before a budget gets pointed at the enterprise version.

  Naming the bad-fit buyer feels redundant inside a company where everyone already knows it. It is also the line used most directly downstream: exclusions, negative keywords, and the qualifying question on a form all come from it, and none of them can be built from a description of who the product is for.
</Accordion>

### 3. What we sell

**Group what the company sells by the job the buyer is trying to get done. Use the buyer's grouping, unless the internal product names are the words people actually search for.**

| Group | What's in it | The job the buyer is doing |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| _E.g. Build_ | _E.g. Process templates, form builder, approval steps_ | _E.g. Get a process out of a spreadsheet and into something that runs_ |
| _E.g. Run_ | _E.g. Scheduled runs, reminders, escalation rules_ | _E.g. Stop chasing people to do their part_ |
| _E.g. Report_ | _E.g. Run history, cycle time, bottleneck view_ | _E.g. Answer "where is this stuck?" without asking three people_ |
| \[add group\] |  |  |

**Which group carries the ad budget, and what makes it the one to spend on?**

_E.g. Build. It is the group with search demand behind it, because people look for a way out of a spreadsheet. Run and Report get bought later, after the first process is live._

<Accordion title="Concept review: What we sell">
  A product list can be organized by what the company built or by what the buyer came to do, and the two rarely line up. Internal grouping follows the engineering roadmap and the pricing page. Buyer grouping follows the problem that made them open a browser tab.

  The choice has a real consequence, because this grouping tends to become the [search theme](/google-search-theme-brief) set and the ad group structure. Grouping by internal product name works when those names are what people type, which happens in categories with established vocabulary. It works badly when the name is invented, since nobody searches for a word only the company uses.

  Naming the group that carries the budget is a separate call from naming the biggest group by revenue. The largest line of business is sometimes the one that sells through partners or renewals, where paid media has the least to do with it.
</Accordion>

### 4. Problems solved

**List the problems customers describe, phrased the way they say them. One row per problem.**

| Problem, in the customer's words | How the product solves it | Where we hear it |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| _E.g. "The spreadsheet broke again and nobody knows who changed what"_ | _E.g. Every change is logged against a person, and the process keeps running_ | _E.g. Sales calls, in 8 of the last 10_ |
| _E.g. "I spend Monday chasing people to do their bit"_ | _E.g. Reminders and escalation run without anyone sending them_ | _E.g. Support tickets, churn interviews_ |
| _E.g. "I can't tell my manager where the process is stuck"_ | _E.g. Run history shows the step and the person it is sitting with_ | _E.g. Win/loss notes_ |
| \[add problem\] |  |  |

<Accordion title="Concept review: Problems solved">
  This section feeds ad copy more directly than any other on the page, which is why the phrasing matters as much as the content. A problem written in the company's vocabulary comes out of the other end as an ad about suboptimal process utilization. The same problem in the customer's vocabulary comes out as an ad about the spreadsheet nobody wants to open on a Monday.

  Where the wording comes from is the practical part. Sales calls, support tickets, churn interviews, and review sites all carry the customer's own words, and they are cheaper to collect than they look. A single re-read of ten recent tickets usually produces better phrasing than a workshop.

  Recording where each problem was heard also tells you how much weight it carries. A problem raised in eight of ten sales calls and a problem mentioned once by a large customer both belong on the list, and they justify very different amounts of budget.
</Accordion>

### 5. Why customers choose us

**List three to five things customers name as the reason they picked this company. One line each on what it means in practice.**

| Reason | What it means in practice |
| :-- | :-- |
| _E.g. Live the same day_ | _E.g. Median time from signup to a first running process is 3.5 hours_ |
| _E.g. No admin needed_ | _E.g. 71% of accounts are created by the process owner, not by IT_ |
| _E.g. It survives contact with a real team_ | _E.g. A named customer ran 400 processes in their first month_ |
| \[add reason\] |  |

**In one sentence, what is the reason customers give most often?**

_E.g. They can have the thing working before they have to justify buying it._

<Accordion title="Concept review: Why customers choose us">
  What sits here is the internal, unpolished answer: the reasons customers actually give, in whatever words they gave them. The market-facing sentence built from those reasons is the value proposition, and it lives in [messaging](/messaging) where it can be reviewed and approved as copy.

  Keeping the two apart is what stops this page from being rewritten every time a headline changes. Reasons customers give move slowly, because they follow what the product does. Approved wording moves faster, because it follows campaigns, audiences, and whatever the last test showed.

  There is a useful test for whether a reason belongs here at all. If every competitor in the category could put the same line on their homepage, it is describing the category rather than the company, and it will not survive the comparison a buyer runs.
</Accordion>

### 6. Competitors

**List the alternatives a buyer is genuinely weighing: same buyer, same problem. Include the option of changing nothing.**

| Name | URL | Note |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| _E.g. Full workflow platforms_ | _E.g. example-platform.com_ | _E.g. Wins any deal that hinges on integration count. Their copy is strong on depth_ |
| _E.g. Spreadsheets_ | _E.g. n/a_ | _E.g. Free and already open. The real incumbent in most deals_ |
| _E.g. Doing nothing_ | _E.g. n/a_ | _E.g. Loses only when the cost of the current process gets concrete_ |
| \[add competitor\] |  |  |

**Which of these turns up most often in deals?**

_E.g. Spreadsheets, by a distance. A named platform appears in maybe one deal in five._

<Accordion title="Concept review: Competitors">
  Three competitor lists exist across this knowledge base and they answer different questions. This one records who a buyer is weighing. The [competitive frame](/positioning#2-competitive-frame) in positioning records which category the brand wants to be judged inside. A [battle card](/competitor-battle-card) is written for one named rival a rep is about to face on a call.

  The trap in building this list is category thinking. Two products can sit in the same market report and never appear in the same deal, because they are sold to different buyers at different price points. The test that keeps the list honest is whether the same person, solving the same problem, would seriously consider both.

  Doing nothing is the entry most often left off, and for many categories it takes the largest share of decisions. It also loses to a different argument. A named competitor loses to being better, and the current situation loses to becoming expensive enough to act on.
</Accordion>

## Optional sections

### A. How it works

<Tip>
  Optional: include if someone writing about the product would otherwise have to guess at the mechanism
</Tip>

**Walk through what happens, from the moment someone signs up or installs to the point where they get something back. Three to six steps, in plain words.**

1. _E.g. The process owner picks a template or builds a form._
2. _E.g. They add the steps and who each one goes to._
3. _E.g. They turn it on, and the first run starts from a form submission or a schedule._
4. _E.g. Run history fills in from the first run onward._

<Accordion title="Concept review: How it works">
  A copywriter who does not know the mechanism writes around it, and writing around a mechanism produces the vague language the category is already full of. Knowing that the first run starts from a form submission is what lets a headline say something a competitor cannot copy.

  The mechanism is also where objections get answered. Most hesitation before a purchase is a practical question about what has to happen first: what gets installed, what access is needed, who has to be involved. Steps written plainly answer those questions before a prospect has to ask.

  Steps stack up quickly here. This section describes the path a customer takes, and a step that only matters to the engineering team pushes the useful ones out of view.
</Accordion>

### B. Integrations

<Tip>
  Optional: include if the product connects to other tools, and buyers ask about it
</Tip>

**List what the product connects to. Names are usually enough.**

_E.g. Slack, HubSpot, Google Sheets, Xero, Zapier._

**Is there one integration that closes deals on its own?**

_E.g. HubSpot. Roughly a third of trials mention it before they sign up._

<Accordion title="Concept review: Integrations">
  An integration list looks like a feature list and behaves like a keyword list. A buyer already running a tool searches for the pair, and that search carries more intent than a category term does, because the person running it has already decided what they use.

  Website logo walls are an unreliable source for this section. They show partners with a logo agreement, which is a different set from what the product connects to, in both directions.

  One integration occasionally decides a purchase on its own. Knowing which one changes what a campaign leads with, because a connection that closes deals belongs in a headline and the rest belong in a list.
</Accordion>

### C. Product and device features

<Tip>
  Optional: include if there is hardware, a spec sheet, or a durability claim
</Tip>

**List the build or technical qualities that a buyer asks about. Short lines, no framing.**

- _E.g. Waterproof to IP67_
- _E.g. Drop-tested to 1.5 metres_
- _E.g. Battery lasts 18 months in the field_
- \[add feature\]

<Accordion title="Concept review: Product and device features">
  Specs are the one part of this page that can be checked by someone outside the company, which makes them the part that carries the most risk when they are approximate. A rounded-up number in an internal document reappears in an ad, and an ad claim that a competitor can disprove is a different kind of problem from a vague one.

  These lines also age on their own schedule. A spec changes with a hardware revision rather than with a marketing decision, so a document that is otherwise current can carry a number for a device that stopped shipping two years ago.

  Where the number came from is worth a few extra words: a spec sheet, a certification, or a test someone ran. It is the difference between a claim that can be defended and one that gets pulled during legal review.
</Accordion>

### D. Feature deep-dive

<Tip>
  Optional: include if one feature is doing most of the selling, or a launch needs its own space
</Tip>

**Which feature earns its own section, and what does it do?**

_E.g. Escalation rules. When a step sits unactioned past its deadline, it moves to a named backup and the owner gets told._

**What does it work on, and where does it stop?**

_E.g. Any step with a single owner. It does not apply to steps assigned to a group, which is the request that comes up most._

<Accordion title="Concept review: Feature deep-dive">
  Most features belong inside the group they sit in, in section 3. A feature earns its own section when it changes the argument: it is the reason a segment buys, or it is new enough that nobody writing copy has seen it work.

  What the feature does not do belongs here as much as what it does. Limits reach copy either way, and finding them in a document is cheaper than finding them in a support ticket after a campaign has been running for three weeks.

  A feature that has earned this section once does not keep it forever. Sections written for a launch tend to outlive the launch, and a differentiator that competitors have since matched reads as a strange emphasis to anyone reading the page a year later.
</Accordion>

### E. Data and reporting

<Tip>
  Optional: include if reporting is part of what customers are buying
</Tip>

**What does the product measure, and at what level does it report?**

| Level | What it shows | Report customers actually open |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| _E.g. Single run_ | _E.g. Every step, who held it, how long it sat_ | _E.g. Stuck runs_ |
| _E.g. Process_ | _E.g. Cycle time, volume, where runs stall_ | _E.g. Monthly cycle time_ |
| _E.g. Account_ | _E.g. Volume across every process, by team_ | _E.g. Rarely opened_ |
| \[add level\] |  |  |

<Accordion title="Concept review: Data and reporting">
  Reporting overlaps with what the company sells, so this section only earns separate space when the reporting is part of the reason people buy.

  The level a product reports at is the detail that gets lost most often, and it decides whether a claim lands. "Full visibility" is a category phrase. "You can see which step a run is sitting on and who has had it since Tuesday" is the same product described at a level a buyer can picture.

  Which report customers open is a separate fact from which reports exist, and the gap between them is usually large. The opened one is the one that belongs in a screenshot.
</Accordion>

### F. Geography and service area

<Tip>
  Optional: include if the company cannot or will not sell everywhere
</Tip>

**Where can the company sell, and where can it not?**

| Area | Can we sell there? | Note |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| _E.g. US, Canada_ | _E.g. Yes_ | _E.g. Strongest conversion rate. Support hours cover both_ |
| _E.g. UK, Ireland, Australia_ | _E.g. Yes_ | _E.g. Support overlaps for four hours a day_ |
| _E.g. EU_ | _E.g. No_ | _E.g. No data residency option yet. Exclude from targeting_ |
| \[add area\] |  |  |

<Accordion title="Concept review: Geography and service area">
  This section turns into targeting settings more directly than anything else on the page. Countries, regions, and exclusions get typed into a campaign, and an area listed here by mistake becomes spend against people who cannot buy.

  Three different limits tend to get collapsed into one answer, and they behave differently. There is where the company is legally able to sell, where it can support a customer once they buy, and where it currently wants to spend. A market can be open on the first two and deliberately closed on the third, which is a decision worth recording as a decision rather than as a limit.

  Regions that convert differently earn a note in the same table. It is the kind of detail that reaches a budget split months later, when nobody remembers where the observation came from.
</Accordion>
