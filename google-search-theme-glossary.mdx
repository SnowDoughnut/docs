title: "Search theme glossary"
description: "Definitions for the terms used across the search theme brief, template, and register, including the difference between our search themes and Google's Performance Max search themes."
---

Terms are listed alphabetically. These are the terms specific to planning and running search themes. Terms used across the whole framework live in the [main glossary](/glossary).

### Ad group negative

A negative keyword applied to one ad group rather than to the whole campaign or account. The instrument that keeps two neighbouring themes out of each other's auctions, because it blocks a term inside one theme without affecting anything else. Recorded in the boundaries section of the [search theme brief](/google-search-theme-brief).

### Ad relevance

One of the three components of [Quality Score](#quality-score). Google defines it as how closely an ad matches the intent behind a search. Google's stated fix for a low rating is to organize keywords by theme and split ad groups holding mismatched keywords, which is the same test as the [one-ad test](#one-ad-test).

### Broad match

The match type that uses the most signals to read intent, matching queries related in meaning rather than in wording. Google's current account structure guidance pairs it with themed ad groups and Smart Bidding. Which match types a theme runs on is the media buyer's decision.

### Close variant

A query Google treats as equivalent to a keyword: misspellings, plurals, reordered words, and same-meaning phrasings. Close variants loosening over time is one of the reasons a [single keyword ad group](#single-keyword-ad-group-skag) no longer receives a single query.

### Exact match

The match type that matches a keyword and its [close variants](#close-variant). Narrower than [phrase](#phrase-match) or [broad](#broad-match), and no longer restricted to the exact words typed.

### Expected clickthrough rate

One of the three components of [Quality Score](#quality-score): how likely an ad is to be clicked when it is shown. Google's guidance notes that an ad mentioning what the person searched for reads as more relevant than one that does not, which is why a theme records a phrase for its ads to carry.

### Intent statement

The one sentence in a [search theme brief](/google-search-theme-brief#1-the-intent) describing what every searcher in the theme is trying to do. Distinct from a topic: "gold" is a topic, "find today's gold price" is an intent. Every later decision in the theme is checked against it.

### Keyword cannibalization

Two of your own ad groups or campaigns competing for the same query, so Google chooses between your own ads and the conversion data for that query splits across both. The failure mode that theme [boundaries](#theme-boundary) exist to prevent.

### Landing page experience

One of the three components of [Quality Score](#quality-score): how relevant and useful the landing page is to someone who clicked. Google's stated fix is to deliver content matching the search and keep messaging consistent from ad to landing page. See [message match](#message-match).

### Match type

The setting that controls how closely a query must relate to a keyword before it can trigger an ad. [Exact](#exact-match), [phrase](#phrase-match), and [broad](#broad-match). Match type policy for a campaign is set in the [Google campaign brief](/google-campaign-brief); which types a specific keyword runs on is the media buyer's call.

### Message match

The continuity between the query, the ad, and the landing page. Not repeating the ad headline on the page word for word, but making the promise the ad made the first thing the page addresses. The [search theme brief](/google-search-theme-brief#5-landing-page) records the phrase both have to carry.

### One-ad test

The test that decides where a theme stops: could one responsive search ad be genuinely relevant to every keyword in the group? If writing a headline that suits one keyword makes it wrong for another, the group is two themes. More reliable than any keyword count. See [search theme concepts](/google-search-theme-concepts#the-one-ad-test-decides-the-boundary).

### Phrase match

The match type that matches queries including the meaning of the keyword, with additional words before or after. Sits between [exact](#exact-match) and [broad](#broad-match).

### Quality Score

Google's diagnostic estimate of ad quality, built from three components: [expected clickthrough rate](#expected-clickthrough-rate), [ad relevance](#ad-relevance), and [landing page experience](#landing-page-experience). A theme touches all three, which is why it holds the keywords, the copy, and the landing page as one unit rather than three separate decisions.

### Responsive search ad

The Search ad format that assembles its own combinations from a pool of headlines and descriptions. Because any individual asset can be left out of a given serve, a phrase a theme depends on is only guaranteed to appear if it is [pinned](/glossary#pinning). See the [Google Search copy template](/google-search-copy-template#pinning).

### Search term

What a person actually typed, as opposed to the keyword that matched it. Keywords are what you bid on; search terms are what you receive. The gap between the two is where a theme drifts.

### Search terms report

The Google Ads report listing the queries that triggered your ads. The place a boundary problem becomes visible, because the same search term appearing under two themes means those themes are competing.

### Search theme

In this framework, one search intent held together as a single unit of planning: the intent statement, the keywords that express it, the customer profile behind them, the landing page they go to, and the ads written to match. On Google Search it becomes one ad group. Not the same as [Google's search themes](#search-themes-performance-max). See [search theme concepts](/google-search-theme-concepts).

### Search themes (Performance Max)

A Google Ads field inside a Performance Max asset group where you add words and phrases customers use to find you. Google allows up to 50 per asset group (raised from 25 in 2025) and gives them the same prioritization as phrase and broad match keywords in a Search campaign. Exclusions and negative keywords still apply, and semantic duplicates such as "car" and "automobile" reach the same audience.

This is a platform input, not a planning document. Our [search theme](#search-theme) is the unit that decides what an ad group targets, who it speaks to, and where it sends them. The two share a name and do different jobs. Checked 13 August 2026.

### Single keyword ad group (SKAG)

An ad group built around one keyword, with its own ads and bid. Once standard practice, now a narrow exception rather than a default: [close variants](#close-variant) loosened, [broad match](#broad-match) improved, and Smart Bidding needs conversion signal pooled rather than split. Still defensible for a single query with enough volume and value to justify its own page and ads, recorded as a deliberate choice.

### Single theme ad group (STAG)

An ad group built around one intent and the several keywords that express it. The industry term for what this framework calls a [search theme](#search-theme) at the platform level.

### Theme boundary

What one theme deliberately does not cover, and which theme covers it instead. Recorded in the [boundaries section](/google-search-theme-brief#3-boundaries) of the brief along with the terms negated at ad group level to enforce it.

### Theme short form

The registered abbreviation for a theme, for example `REPORTAUTO`, used in the ad group name and the `utm_content` value. Comes from the [ad group theme dictionary](/ad-set-group-naming-conventions#ad-group-theme-search-and-shopping). A theme described in prose at build time produces a value no report can group by.

### Themed ad group

An ad group organized around one intent rather than one keyword. Google's current [account structure guidance](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/14752782) recommends it over single-keyword groups, on the reasoning that grouping keywords into similar themes makes it easier to understand the keywords, select the best one, and decide which ad should serve for each query.
