---
title: "Campaign retrospective process"
description: "How a finished campaign gets closed out: results against the target that was set, what actually caused them, what carries forward, and where the record goes so the next campaign can find it."
---

A retrospective is what turns a finished campaign into something the next one can use. Without it, a team runs the same campaign four times and learns from it once, informally, through whoever happens to remember.

Run this after the conversion window has closed, not on the last day of the flight.

## What you'll end up with

- The result against the target that was set before launch.
- A short account of what caused it, separated from what you observed.
- A named decision: repeat, adapt, or stop.
- Anything worth keeping written into the standard it belongs to, not just noted here.

## Before you start

| You need | Where it lives |
| :-- | :-- |
| The brief, with its original target | [Campaigns library](/campaign-library-overview) |
| Final numbers | [Flat file](/flat-file-template), after the conversion window closes |
| The change log | Whatever recorded budget and creative changes mid-flight |
| Any test results from the campaign | [Experiment learning library](/experiment-learning-home) |

<Warning>
  Pull the target from the filed brief, not from memory. A retrospective that judges a campaign against a number recalled after seeing the results is not a retrospective.
</Warning>

## The process

<Steps>
  <Step title="1. State the result against the original target">
    Primary KPI, actual against target, and the counter-metric alongside it. Spend against budget, from [budget reporting](/budget-reporting-process).

    No interpretation yet. Just the numbers and what was promised.
  </Step>
  <Step title="2. Read the change log before explaining anything">
    Budget shifts, creative swaps, audience changes, tracking fixes. Most of what looks like a performance story has a recorded cause, and attributing a mid-flight budget move to creative quality is the classic retrospective error.
  </Step>
  <Step title="3. Separate what you observed from what you think caused it">
    Write them as different sentences, so someone can accept your numbers and dispute your explanation.

    _E.g. "Cost per trial finished at \$52 against a \$45 target. Two of the three creative concepts stopped delivering after week two, and spend concentrated into the third, which had the highest cost per trial of the set."_
  </Step>
  <Step title="4. Name what you would do differently, specifically">
    "Better creative" is not a finding. "Three concepts was too few for a six-week flight at this budget" is, because the next brief can act on it.

    Be equally specific about what worked. Retrospectives skew toward problems, and a team that only records failures slowly forgets what it is good at.
  </Step>
  <Step title="5. Decide: repeat, adapt, or stop">
    One of the three, named explicitly, with the condition attached.

    | Decision | Means |
    | :-- | :-- |
    | Repeat | Run it again as-is, at the same or higher budget |
    | Adapt | Run it again with the named changes from step 4 |
    | Stop | Do not run this again. Record why, so it is not proposed afresh next quarter |
  </Step>
  <Step title="6. Write findings into the standards they belong to">
    This is the step that makes the difference, and the one most often skipped.

    A copy insight goes into the [copy brief](/copy-brief) or [copy best practices](/copy-best-practices). A structural lesson goes into [campaign structure best practices](/campaign-structure-best-practices). A budget assumption that proved wrong goes into [budget planning](/budget-planning). A finding that stays in a retrospective is a finding nobody applies.
  </Step>
  <Step title="7. Link it back and file it">
    Attach the retrospective to the campaign brief in the [campaigns library](/campaign-library-overview), so the brief carries its own outcome. That's what makes the library worth searching before the next campaign.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Keep it short

A retrospective long enough to feel thorough is usually too long to be read. One page is enough for the result, the cause, the decision, and what changed as a result. The detail is already in the reports.

## Exceptions

A campaign stopped early still gets a retrospective, and it is usually the more useful one. Record why it was stopped and at what point that became clear, since recognising a failing campaign sooner is itself the lesson.

Always-on activity gets a retrospective on a set cadence rather than at an end date, most naturally alongside the [quarterly report](/quarterly-reporting-process).
