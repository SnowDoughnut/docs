A copy brief is the shared starting point for a single piece of writing: an email, a search ad, a social caption, a landing page section, an SMS message. It records what that piece has to say and do (the reader, the message, the proof, the required elements) so a writer can draft it without guessing, and so the same piece can be judged against something specific once it's done.

This page explains the concepts behind each part of the brief: what a section is for, and what tends to go wrong when it's skipped or left vague. It isn't the fill-in template. Use it to understand the thinking, then fill in the [copy brief](/copy-brief).

## Copy brief and creative brief are not the same document

The two get used interchangeably, and on a small team the same person often writes both. But they're answering different questions. A creative brief sets the concept: the look, the feel, the visual direction, and the strategic core (problem, objective, customer profile) that everything downstream has to follow. A copy brief works one level down from that. It assumes the strategy already exists and asks a narrower question: given that strategy, what does this specific piece of writing need to say, in what order, and within what constraints?

That's why a copy brief leans on links rather than redefining things. The objective, the customer profile, and the core message usually already live in a linked campaign or creative brief. Retyping them here would just create a second version that can drift from the first. What a copy brief adds on top is the part a creative brief doesn't usually cover: the exact wording constraints, the channel-by-channel character limits, the hook, and the specific objections this piece of writing has to answer.

## Why a copy brief needs to be flexible about channel

Ad copy standards used to describe headline, body, and CTA copy for ads specifically. That's too narrow for what a writer actually gets asked to produce. The same campaign message might need to become a 125-character Meta primary text, a 60-character email subject line, a 160-character SMS, and a search headline capped at 30 characters, all in the same week, all carrying the same underlying idea.

A copy brief that assumes one channel breaks the moment a second one shows up. The version here treats the strategic core (objective, customer profile, message, voice, mandatories) as channel-agnostic, and pushes everything channel-specific (exact character limits, field structure, format) down into a per-channel template that the brief links to rather than restates. That split is what keeps the brief itself short even as the number of channels it has to serve grows.

## The strategic core

### Objective and reader action

A single objective line usually collapses two different things: the result the business wants, and the thing you're actually asking one specific reader to do. "Increase trials" doesn't tell a writer anything about what to put in an email. "Read this subject line and open the email" does.

Splitting the objective into a business goal, a communication objective, and a reader action keeps the commercial ambition from getting handed to a writer as if it were direction. The reader action is the layer a writer can actually build toward, and it only exists if someone writes it down.

### Customer profile and insight

Most copy briefs stop at who the reader is: their role, their age range, their company size. That tells a media buyer where to run the ad. It tells a writer almost nothing about what to say once the ad is in front of them.

The insight is the missing half: what this reader currently believes, or doubts, or has been burned by, that the copy has to work against. A profile description places someone in a bucket. An insight gives a writer a specific belief to push on. Pulling the profile itself from a shared library, rather than redescribing it in every brief, is also what keeps every piece of copy written for the same reader consistent with every other one.

### Key message and proof

The message is the one idea the reader should leave with, not a list of every true thing the copy could say. It should come from the campaign or creative brief this copy serves, since a campaign where every asset carries a slightly different "main" message stops reading like one campaign.

Proof is why the message is worth believing rather than just asserted. A number, a specific mechanism, a demonstration, or a customer's own words all do more work than an adjective. Copy that states a benefit with nothing behind it is asking for trust it hasn't earned yet; copy with a proof point next to the claim is offering a reason instead.

### Voice, tone, and mandatories

Voice and tone live in one document, the [brand voice and tone workbook](/brand-voice-and-tone), so this section's job in a copy brief is narrow: point to the part of that document that applies to this specific piece, not restate the rules. A pricing email and a support reply might draw on different sections of the same workbook.

Mandatories are the fixed edges: what must appear, and what must never appear, kept in two separate lists rather than one paragraph. A required legal line and a stylistic preference read very differently to a writer when they're not sitting in the same sentence. Separating them makes the non-negotiables impossible to miss and makes everything else visibly open.

### Channels and deliverables

This is where a copy brief commits to which channels this particular piece runs on, and hands each one off to its own template. The reasoning for the hand-off is simple: character limits and field structure are specific to a platform and change when the platform changes its ad specs, so keeping that detail in one template per channel, rather than copied into every brief that uses it, means it only has to be updated in one place.

### Success criteria

Copy can be judged two ways, and they're easy to blur. Copy acceptance asks whether the writing itself is right: on-brand, within the character limits, carrying every required element. Campaign performance asks whether it worked once it was live: click-through, conversion, reply rate.

Both belong in the brief, even for a writer who isn't accountable for the performance number, because knowing what's being measured shapes what gets written. A writer told the subject line is judged on open rate will write it differently than one only told to "sound on-brand."

## Sections that earn their place conditionally

Three concepts matter for some copy and not for others. Forcing them into every brief adds length without adding direction; the question for each is the same one that governs every optional section in this system: would answering it change a word of what gets written?

| Concept | Include it when |
| --- | --- |
| Hook and opening line direction | The reader's attention has to be earned before anything else gets read: a scroll-stopping ad, a subject line, the first seconds of a script |
| Objections the copy must answer | The reader is likely to hesitate before taking the action named in the objective |
| Localization and reading level | The copy runs across regions, languages, or has to hold to a specific reading level |

The hook deserves a specific note, since it's one of the most channel-dependent decisions in the whole brief. What earns attention in a cold paid-social scroll (a pattern interrupt, a bold claim) is not what earns it in an inbox someone already opens on purpose (relevance, specificity). Naming the job the opening line has to do, rather than just asking for something "punchy," gives a writer a target instead of a mood.

## Supporting material and channel templates

A copy brief links out to two different kinds of document, and they serve different purposes. The brand system (voice, messaging, positioning, assets, approved claims) is stable and shared across everything anyone writes, so the brief points to it instead of copying it in. The channel templates are the opposite: each one is the actual production spec for a single format, where the real character limits, headline counts, and field structure live.

That split is what lets one copy brief describe a piece of writing that has to become five different pieces of copy without turning into five different documents. The strategy is written once, at the top. Each channel just needs a template filled in underneath it.
