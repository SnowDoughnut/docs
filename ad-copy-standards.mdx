## 1. Objective and reader action

**Fill in all three so a business number never lands on a writer's desk as if it were direction:**

| Level | Fill in |
| :-- | :-- |
| Business goal | _E.g. Grow self-serve trial signups 20% this quarter._ |
| Communication objective | _E.g. Make setup feel fast and low-risk to someone who's never used the product._ |
| Reader action | _E.g. Reads the email and starts a trial._ |

<Accordion title="Concept review: Objective and reader action">
  The business goal is the commercial result. The communication objective is what this specific piece of copy has to change in the reader's head. The reader action is the concrete thing you want them to do once they've read, watched, or heard it.

  Collapsing these into one line usually loses the part a writer can act on. "Increase trials" doesn't tell anyone what to write; "reads the subject line and opens the email" does.
</Accordion>

## 3. Key message and proof

**State the one idea the reader should take away, and the reasons they should believe it:**

**Core message:** _E.g. Doughnut Labs gets you live in a day, not a quarter, and you can prove it before you pay a cent._

**Supporting proof:**

- _E.g. Guided 4-step setup, no sales call required_
- _E.g. Median time-to-first-ship under 3 hours_
- _E.g. 2,000\+ teams live_
- \[add proof point\]

<Accordion title="Concept review: Key message and proof">
  This message should come from the campaign brief's messaging, not get reinvented for this one piece. When two assets in the same campaign carry two different "most important" messages, the campaign stops reading like one campaign.

  Proof is what makes the message credible instead of just asserted. A number, a specific mechanism, a demonstration, or a customer's own words all do more work than an adjective.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Concept review: Channels and deliverables">
  The same message needs a different shape on every channel: a different length, a different structure, a different idea of what counts as the call to action. Listing every channel this copy runs on is what stops a version written for one from getting reused unchanged on another.

  This table doesn't repeat each channel's character limits, since the linked template already carries them and a second copy would just be one more place for the numbers to go stale. Its job is only to say which channels apply and route the writer to the right starting point.

## 6. Success criteria

**Define both. Fill the type that applies:**

**Copy acceptance (is the writing itself right?):**

- _E.g. Uses the approved CTA wording exactly_
- _E.g. Fits every channel's character limits_
- _E.g. Matches brand voice section 4_
- \[add criterion\]

**Campaign performance (did it do its job?):**

- _E.g. Email click-through above 3%_
- _E.g. Meta CTR above 1.2%_
- \[add metric\]

<Accordion title="Concept review: Success criteria">
  Copy acceptance asks whether the writing is right on its own terms: on-brand, within limits, carrying the required elements. Campaign performance asks whether it moved a number once it ran.

  Both belong here even if the writer isn't accountable for the performance number, because knowing what's being measured changes what gets written. A writer who knows click-through is the bar will write a different subject line than one who was only told to sound on-brand.
</Accordion>

## Part 2: Optional sections

<Tip>
  Add a section here only when it would change what gets written. A section that wouldn't change a word adds length without direction.
</Tip>

### Hook and opening line direction

<Tip>
  Optional: include when the reader's attention has to be earned before anything else gets read, such as a paid social ad, an email subject line, or the first seconds of a video script.
</Tip>

**What should the first line, subject, or opening moment do?**

_E.g. Open with the specific objection ("Setup looks like it'll eat a week") rather than opening with the product name._

<Accordion title="Concept review: Hook and opening line direction">
  A hook is judged by whether it earns the next line, not by whether it's clever on its own. Naming the job it should do (open a curiosity gap, name a pain point, state a specific promise) gives a writer something more precise to aim at than "make it punchy."

  What earns attention varies by channel and by reader. A cold paid-social scroll and a subject line in an inbox someone already trusts are different problems, which is why this section exists separately from the channel table above rather than being folded into it.
</Accordion>

### Objections the copy must answer

<Tip>
  Optional: include when the reader is likely to hesitate before taking the action named in section 1.
</Tip>

**What might make the reader hesitate, and how does the copy answer it?**

| Objection | How the copy answers it |
| :-- | :-- |
| _E.g. "Is my data safe during the trial?"_ | _E.g. SOC 2 badge and a one-line data policy near the CTA_ |
| \[add row\] |  |

<Accordion title="Concept review: Objections the copy must answer">
  Listing the doubt next to its answer turns a vague instruction like "make it persuasive" into work a writer can actually do. Which objections matter depends on the reader and the channel: a warm email list and a cold search ad usually carry different doubts.

  Not every piece of copy needs this section. A short brand-awareness line with no direct ask may not be answering an objection at all.
</Accordion>

### Localization and reading level

<Tip>
  Optional: include when the copy runs across regions, languages, or needs to hold to a specific reading level.
</Tip>

**What localization or reading-level requirements apply?**

_E.g. Localize for UK and Canada. Allow roughly 30% text expansion for the German version. Keep support-adjacent copy around a grade 6 reading level._

<Accordion title="Concept review: Localization and reading level">
  A character limit that fits comfortably in English can overflow once a line is translated, and a headline that reads clearly at one reading level can lose part of its audience at another. Naming these requirements before drafting starts lets a writer build around them instead of cutting a translated line down after the fact.

  This matters most for copy that's public-facing or reaches a broad or international reader. A single internal email to an English-speaking team may not need this section at all.
</Accordion>
