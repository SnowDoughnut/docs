A visual hook is what the viewer sees in the first second, before a word lands. It works alongside the spoken line and the text on screen, which come from [hook types](/hook-types). A video ad opens with one of each: one visual, one spoken line, one line of text.

A face to camera already stops the scroll. A visual hook adds movement or a reveal in that first second, so the face arrives with a reason to keep watching.

## The hooks

| # | Hook | What the viewer sees | How to film it |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Slide into frame | The speaker enters an empty frame from the side and starts talking | Lock the camera on an empty shot, step in from one edge on the first word |
| 2 | Quick zoom out | A tight shot snaps wide to reveal the speaker or the setting | Start on a close detail, zoom or cut wide inside half a second |
| 3 | Random object as a mic | The speaker talks into something that is not a microphone | Hold a hairbrush, banana, or the product itself like a mic, played straight |
| 4 | Laptop turn | The speaker spins a laptop toward the camera to show the screen | Start with the lid facing the speaker, turn it on the first line |
| 5 | Camera pull back | The shot backs away to show more than the viewer expected | Start close, walk the camera back or pull out on a gimbal |
| 6 | Talk in front of the subject | The speaker stands in front of the thing they are talking about | Frame the product, place, or result behind the speaker's shoulder |
| 7 | Wired earphone mic | The speaker holds a wired earphone up like a lapel mic | Hold the earphone's mic near the mouth, as in street interviews |
| 8 | Camera rotate | The frame turns as the clip opens | Rotate the phone or gimbal 90 to 180 degrees into the opening shot |
| 9 | Close-up stack montage | Three or four fast close-ups before the speaker appears | Shoot tight detail clips, cut each to well under a second |
| 10 | Object metaphor | A prop stands in for the problem | Show the prop doing the problem: a leaking bucket for wasted spend |
| 11 | Split-screen whiteboard | The speaker on one side, a whiteboard sketch on the other | Film the speaker and the board separately, place them side by side |
| 12 | Physical timer countdown | A real timer starts as the speaker begins | Set a kitchen timer or phone stopwatch in frame and start it on the first word |
| 13 | Unrelated action while talking | The speaker talks while doing something ordinary | Cook, fold laundry, or do makeup while delivering the script |
| 14 | Driving and talking | The speaker talks from a car seat | Film parked or as a passenger, never while driving |
| 15 | Walk towards camera and talk | The speaker walks into a close shot while already talking | Start far back, walk toward a fixed camera, land in frame on the key line |

Yapper ads add four small openings of their own, filmed face to camera: hands coming together without a clap, putting the hair up or down, getting into the car and shutting the door, and propping the phone up as recording starts. They sit in the [Yapper Framework](/skill-yapper-framework#the-hook).

## Picking one

One visual hook per video. Variants of a video are different visual hooks on the same script, or different spoken hooks on the same visual, never both at once, so a test shows which change did the work.

The visual has to make sense with the spoken line. A countdown timer opening a story about a slow, gradual change confuses the viewer; the same timer opening "you have until Friday" lands.

Two guardrails hold whatever the hook:

- Film driving shots parked or from the passenger seat.
- A countdown or other urgency cue has to match a real deadline. A timer on an evergreen offer is the kind of claim [legal review](/legal-review-process) exists to catch.
