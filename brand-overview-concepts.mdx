The [brand overview](/brand-overview) records what is true about a business. [Positioning](/positioning) decides what to claim about it. Teams that keep one document for both end up with a page that is part description and part argument, where nobody can tell which lines were checked and which were decided in a meeting.

This page explains what the overview layer holds and why it sits underneath the rest of the brand system. It isn't a fill-in workbook. Read it before filling in the overview, or when a fact in it turns out to be wrong.

## What the overview layer holds

Four things, all checkable: what the company sells, who buys it today, what problem those buyers are solving, and what else they are considering. Everything in it either came from a person at the company or from a page someone opened.

That constraint is what makes the layer useful. A document where every line is traceable can be handed to a new hire, a freelancer, or an agency, and the questions that come back are about the business rather than about which parts of the document to trust.

## Description and decision fail differently

Positioning can be wrong. A target can be the wrong target, a competitive frame can be a shelf nobody would ever look on, and no amount of care in the writing will show it. Those are decisions, and they get tested in market.

An overview cannot be wrong in that sense. It can only be out of date, or unsourced. Those are the two failure modes, and both are caught by looking rather than by testing: the product page says something the document doesn't, or a line has no name and no date next to it.

Keeping the layers apart follows from that difference. They are reviewed by different people, on different triggers, and merging them means the checkable half gets reopened every time the arguable half is debated.

| Layer | What it does | Reviewed when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Brand overview](/brand-overview) | Records what the business sells, to whom, against what alternatives | The product, the market, or the competitor set changes |
| [Positioning](/positioning) | Chooses the target, the frame, the difference, and the evidence | The category or the buyer changes |
| [Messaging](/messaging) | Fixes the words that carry the claim | The claim moves, or a proof point expires |

## Every line has a source

There are two legitimate sources for a line in the overview: someone at the company said it, or it was read on a page that was actually opened. Nothing else qualifies, including inference from what similar products do.

The reason for the rule is that a guess and a confirmed fact look identical once they are written down in the same font. Whoever reads the document next has no way to tell them apart, and the guess gets treated as settled. It reaches a search theme, then an ad, then a sales call, where the person defending it discovers it was never true.

The failures here look plausible. A benefit the product does not quite deliver, a customer type that would make sense, a feature the category usually has. None of those look like errors on the page. They look like the rest of the document.

Asking costs a message and a few minutes of someone's time. Repeating a fact that turns out to be wrong costs the reader's confidence in every other line on the page.

## Marketing language is already a claim

Company websites describe products in the language of the argument they are making. A site that says it is reimagining how teams work has made a claim, and copying that sentence into the overview puts a claim into the layer that is supposed to hold facts.

The move is to write down what the thing does. A site that reimagines fleet intelligence tracks where equipment is, how long it idles, and what it costs per hour. The second version can be built on: it produces search themes, ad copy, and objection handling. The first version can only be repeated.

This is also the quickest test of whether an overview has been filled in properly. Read a section and ask what a competitor would have to change to say something different. When the answer is nothing, the section is carrying the category's language rather than the company's facts.

## Three competitor lists, three jobs

Competitors appear in three places in this knowledge base, and the lists are not interchangeable.

- **The overview list** records who a buyer is genuinely weighing, including the option of changing nothing. It is descriptive, and it is the widest of the three.
- **The [competitive frame](/positioning#2-competitive-frame)** records which category the brand wants to be judged inside. It is a decision, and it changes which comparisons a buyer runs.
- **A [battle card](/competitor-battle-card)** is written for one named rival that shows up in deals often enough to earn the maintenance.

Confusing the first two is the common case. A brand lists every company in its market report as a competitor, then writes messaging against all of them, and produces copy that answers questions no buyer was asking.

## What reads from this page

The overview is worth maintaining because other documents pull from it instead of restating it.

| Document | Pulls from the overview |
| --- | --- |
| [Customer profile brief](/customer-profile-brief) | Who buys today, how they buy, and the buyer to exclude |
| [Google search theme brief](/google-search-theme-brief) | The grouping of what the company sells, and the problems in the customer's words |
| [Copy brief](/copy-brief) | The problems, the reasons customers choose, and the mechanism |
| [Marketing campaign brief](/marketing-campaign-brief) | The competitor set and the service area |
| [Competitor battle card](/competitor-battle-card) | The competitors that turned out to matter |

A brief that pastes the facts in creates a second copy, and the second copy is the one still saying the product does not support EU customers six months after it does.

## What ages, and how fast

Sections of the overview decay at different rates, which is why a single annual review tends to miss things.

- **What we sell** changes with the roadmap, and usually without anyone telling marketing.
- **Geography and service area** moves the moment support hours or data residency change, and it carries the most direct cost when it is stale, because it becomes targeting.
- **Competitors** change when someone new starts appearing in deals, which sales notices months before a document does.
- **Specs and numbers** change with a hardware revision or a new data pull, and they carry the most risk because they can be checked by people outside the company.
- **Who we serve** moves slowly, and when it does move it usually means the positioning needs revisiting too.

The practical fix is the name and date recorded in section 1 of the workbook. A document with a person attached to it gets corrected. A document with no author gets worked around.
