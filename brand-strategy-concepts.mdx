Three documents define what the brand says: [positioning](/positioning), [messaging](/messaging), and [brand voice and tone](/brand-voice-and-tone). They're often treated as one exercise, which is why brand strategy work so frequently produces a deck everyone agrees with and nobody can apply.

This page explains what each layer decides and how they fit together. It isn't a fill-in workbook. Read it to understand the reasoning, then fill in the three documents themselves.

## Three layers, three different questions

Each document answers a question the others can't.

| Layer | Question it answers | Changes when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Positioning | What are we claiming, against whom, for whom? | The product, the category, or the buyer changes |
| Messaging | What words do we use to make that claim? | The claim is repositioned, or the evidence behind it moves |
| Voice and tone | How do we sound while saying it? | The brand's personality is deliberately changed, which is rare |

The layers have very different lifespans, which is the practical reason to keep them apart. Positioning should hold for years. Messaging gets revised as proof points update and audiences shift. Tone flexes within a single email. Merging them into one document means the stable part gets edited every time the volatile part does, and nobody can tell which changes were strategic and which were housekeeping.

## Why positioning comes first

Positioning is the only one of the three that can be wrong in a way the others can't detect. Messaging written on top of a weak position reads well and sells nothing, because the sentences are competent and the argument underneath them is the same argument every competitor is making.

The order also determines what a disagreement is about. When a headline gets argued over and the position is written down, the argument resolves against something: does this line support the claim or not? When the position isn't written down, the same argument becomes a contest of taste, and it gets settled by whoever is most senior in the room.

### What a position actually commits to

A position that costs nothing isn't one. Four things have to be named, and three of them involve giving something up:

- **A target**, which means declining to optimize for everyone else.
- **A competitive frame**, which means accepting the comparisons that come with the shelf you chose.
- **A point of difference**, which is only a difference if competitors would dispute it.
- **Reasons to believe**, which is the part that fails quietly when a claim outlives its evidence.

The frame is the least intuitive of the four and often the most consequential. It's the category a buyer files the brand under, and it decides which features get compared and which questions get asked. A brand filed under an established category inherits that category's evaluation criteria whether or not those criteria favor it.

### Doing nothing is usually the largest competitor

Competitive frames tend to list named companies, because those are the ones that show up in sales calls and in win/loss reports. For most categories the biggest alternative is the buyer continuing as they are, and it loses to a different argument than a named competitor does. Beating a competitor requires being better. Beating inaction requires the cost of the current situation to become concrete enough to act on.

Leaving it off the list means the whole messaging set gets built to win comparisons that most of the market never runs.

## What messaging adds on top

Messaging is the position rewritten for someone who doesn't work here. That translation is where most of the value is, and it involves three moves the position doesn't make.

**It changes vocabulary.** A positioning statement is written to be testable and is full of internal shorthand. A value proposition has to survive one read by a distracted person.

**It sets a hierarchy.** The same argument has to work at four lengths: a tagline, a sentence, a spoken pitch, a boilerplate paragraph. A message that only exists at one length gets improvised at the others, and improvisation across a sales team produces several versions of the company.

**It fixes what's provable.** The claims list is where messaging meets evidence. A claim with a source and a refresh date behaves differently from one without, because claims decay in a specific way: the sentence stays the same while the number behind it moves.

### Pillars are a filter, not a list

Message pillars exist to make a brand repeat itself. Recognition accumulates through repetition across a small number of ideas, and it doesn't accumulate at all across a large number.

The cap is the mechanism. Three pillars force a decision about what not to say. Nine pillars accommodate everything, which means the document has stopped rejecting anything and no longer functions as a filter. The specific number matters less than whether the list is closed.

### Varying emphasis is not varying substance

Different audiences care about different things, and the pillars get reordered accordingly. That's ordinary. What breaks a brand is giving each audience its own argument, because the audiences talk to each other and the difference is visible.

The useful discipline is to keep the pillar set fixed and let only the order and the emphasis move. An ops lead hears the speed pillar first. Their manager hears the durability pillar first. Both hear the same three pillars, which is what makes the two conversations recognizably the same company.

## Where voice sits

Voice is the layer people reach for first because it's the most fun to write and the easiest to have an opinion about. It's also the layer that matters least if the two beneath it are missing. A distinctive voice attached to an undifferentiated position produces copy that sounds like something and says nothing.

Voice does real work once the other layers exist. It's what stops a correct message from reading as generic, and it's the only one of the three that shows up in every single piece of output, including error messages and receipts that no strategy document ever anticipated.

The [voice and tone workbook](/brand-voice-and-tone) covers it in full. The relevant point here is sequencing: voice is a treatment applied to a message, and applying a treatment to nothing produces nothing.

## How these documents get used

The three documents are only worth maintaining if downstream work links to them. A campaign brief that pastes in the positioning statement creates a second copy that drifts within a quarter, and after a year nobody can tell which version is current.

| Downstream document | Pulls from |
| --- | --- |
| [Marketing campaign brief](/marketing-campaign-brief) | The pillar this campaign carries, and the audience row it targets |
| [Copy brief](/ad-copy-standards) | The message, the approved claims, the relevant voice section |
| [Creative brief](/creative-brief) | The position, the pillar, and the proof the concept has to make visible |
| [Landing page standards](/landing-page-standards) | The value proposition and the objection responses |

That's what makes the link-don't-copy rule more than a preference. A claim corrected in one place corrects everywhere, and a stat that expires gets caught once, at the source.
